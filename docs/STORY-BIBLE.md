# DeBrief — Story Bible

*Design only. No tree edits, no repo writes. Written 2026-10-30 at Chase's
directive: "write the entire story and lore etc."*

**Status rule for this whole document:** everything traces to
`~/workspace/debrief/DESIGN.md` or `~/workspace/debrief/WORLD.md`, or it is
marked **PROPOSED** — new story or lore that needs Chase's ruling before it
is canon. Nothing in this file silently invents canon. The canon-consistency
appendix (§12) and the proposals list (§13) are part of the document, not
afterthoughts.

**Tone law (from PS15-DIRECTION.md):** PS1.5 presentation — chunky low-poly,
painted surfaces, sodium night grades — but the era binds the pixels, never
the logic. Simulation, AI, and systems are modern and uncompromised. The
story is told the same way: period-true 1987 on the surface, a modern
simulation underneath, and the dread is bureaucratic and moral — a quiet
room, a red light on a recorder, a man reading your own work back to you.

---

## 1. The pitch, as story

1987. The Cold War did not end — it curdled. A homegrown, flag-wrapped
movement took the United States by emergency, election, and decree, until
the takeover was a fact nobody could point to the start of. What remains
of the old republic governs nothing but its own memory. You are an NSA
special agent — a service in exile that kept its ears when it lost its
country — working a drowned, watchtowered Atlantic port city where every
agency on earth runs spies and nobody admits the war is still on.

Each operation is a story the city tells about you. Each operation ends
in a room, under a light, where a man reads back everything you did —
and the file is written forever. The city never resets. The game never
ends. Your record is the game.

The series is **DeBrief**; the first entry is **A City of Spies**. The
player *is* the operator of the banner, career after career: when an
agent is burned — exposed, captured, killed — the career ends and a new
agent arrives, inherits the same city with its same grudges and
unfinished business, and reads the predecessor's file.

## 2. The history of the occupation — how 1987 got here (CANON)

The timeline splits in the late 1970s. Every date below is from WORLD.md
§1; the telling is the bible's, the facts are canon.

- **The Emergency (1979–81).** Economic collapse, energy shocks, and
  street violence let a disciplined, flag-wrapped movement — the
  **Vigilance Party** in its public face — sell the country a single
  product: order. Emergency powers were granted "temporarily," renewed
  annually, then renewed forever.
- **The Quiet Year (1983).** The courts were "streamlined," the press
  was "coordinated," and the last election that mattered was held.
  Nothing burned. That is the horror of this world: it fell in good
  order, with paperwork, and most people were home for dinner.
- **The Settlement (1984– ).** The old federal agencies were absorbed,
  purged, or exiled. The Regime governs through **Administrators** —
  one per district — and rules the streets with its two instruments:
  its soldiery and its mechas.
- **The accelerated century.** The emergency state poured money into
  control technology a generation early: the walking machines, the
  listening networks, an arms program that put the HK USP into Agency
  hands years before its time. The world bible keeps the receipt: the
  USP is a product of the Regime's own rearmament crash programs —
  which is why the exile NSA's standard sidearm is a gun the old
  world never issued.

The curfew in the city is "temporary." It has been temporary for eleven
years running. The city lives under a permanent emergency that nobody
remembers declaring. Watchtowers on the dockyards. Papers checked at
bridges.

**What canon does not say (do not fill silently):** canon names no
individual Vigilance leader, no capital, no foreign war, no date for
the Regime's founding beyond the movement's rise. Any such name is
PROPOSED until Chase rules. The story below is written to work without
them: the enemy is a system, not a villain with a face — which is the
point of this world.

## 3. The Administration, the Vigilance Party, the three bars (CANON)

- **The name it uses:** the **Administration** — always "the
  Administration," as if there had never been another. Its ruling party
  is the Vigilance Party; its uniformed arm in the districts answers to
  the local Administrator.
- **The mark: three white bars on red.** Worn as the soldier's armband,
  painted on the mecha's shoulder, hung as a banner over streets. The
  bars stand for the regime's three founding pledges — **Order, Unity,
  Silence** — in that order, always in that order. Citizens read them
  as a warning; the Agency reads them as a signature: where the three
  bars are, the Administration has been, and has taken the trouble to
  say so.
- **Doctrine: terror as administration.** The soldiery does not merely
  occupy — it raids, makes reprisals, and makes examples, on a schedule
  the districts learn to fear like weather. The mechas are the same
  doctrine rendered in steel: not battlefield weapons (there is no
  battlefield) but instruments of presence — armoured arguments that
  resistance is arithmetic.
- **Its weakness, and the game lives here:** the Administration is a
  bureaucracy. It files everything, trusts its own paperwork, and
  cannot imagine being read. An agency with the best ears in the world
  can live inside its filing system like a worm in an apple.

**The soldiery (CANON).** Occupation troops in doctrine and practice:
terrorist soldiers who rule the districts by raid, reprisal, and
spectacle, hunting the resistance and the Agency alike. Black greatcoat
and pouches over dark armour, blank mask and goggles, suppressed rifle,
red shoulder and red armband bearing the three white bars (golden #4;
rear #7: greatcoat split skirt, back harness, three-bars armband on the
LEFT arm, rifle carried low behind). A conscript, not a machine: he
hesitates, he can be wrong, rain and cold degrade him, and he fears the
mecha — a patrol yields out of its arcs, a tell the player learns to
read like weather. His states — unaware, suspicious, searching,
alarmed — are carried in posture and sound, never in icons.

**The checkpoint system (CANON).** The Regime does not only patrol
Greyport; it processes it. Barriers, a boom gate, a kiosk, a queue of
citizens. The verb is **HALT**: the soldier's raised left palm (golden
#15, rear pose) is the checkpoint's grammar — stop, be seen, be read.
Papers are presented, not clicked; the inspection takes real time, in
the open, under the lamp, while the queue watches. Papers are a system:
identity is layered — face, dress, papers, behaviour. A forgery is a
physical object the Archive makes — it can be good, stale, or for a man
who died last week. The inspector is a conscript with a quota and a
mood; rain, cold, and fear of the mecha degrade him. Failure is
graduated: a bad read is a question, then a detention, then force.
The checkpoint can be avoided (the rooftops are the upper floor),
bribed only with what a conscript risks his life to hold, or passed
clean — and a clean pass is information: you learn the rota, the
inspector, the hour the lamp battery dies. Every crossing, clean or
not, is a line in the debrief. The Archive is built out of crossings.

## 4. The NSA in exile, Control, the Archive, the debrief room (CANON)

- **A service in exile.** What the Settlement could not absorb, it
  scattered — and the listeners scattered best. The exile NSA is a
  remnant network: listening posts in friendly embassies that
  officially do not exist, dead-letter infrastructure older than the
  Regime, and a handful of field assets inside the occupation. It kept
  its ears when it lost its country. It is not the hero of this world;
  it is one player among many, with the best ears and its own dirty
  ledger. The series interrogates the operator's loyalty as hard as
  any target's.
- **Control is not a person so much as a voice and a protocol** — the
  handler on the scrambled line, the man in the debrief room. Whether
  Control is one officer or a duty desk is a question the series
  answers late, if ever. **MYSTERY LAW: whether the Handler is Control
  or only its face is deliberately unstated, and this bible does not
  resolve it.** The voice on the record and the man in the room may or
  may not be one person, and the game does not answer it.
- **The Archive is the war.** The Agency's war is archival: it intends
  to out-remember the Regime. Every op files evidence — names, orders,
  signatures — toward a reckoning the file calls **the Archive**.
  This is why the debrief matters: the player is not just surviving
  the occupation, he is building the case against it, one operation
  at a time.
- **The debrief room as institution.** Every operation ends the same
  way: a small room, a recorder, the handler's voice. The op is read
  back — what you did, what you left, who saw you, what the blood
  says. The game grades the file, not a score screen — **Ghost,
  Surgeon, Butcher, Ghost-That-Never-Was**; the words are the
  Agency's, and they follow you. The service record is the save file:
  career — clearances, reputation with the city's factions, heat,
  commendations, black marks — persists across ops and shapes what
  the city offers and what Control trusts you with.
- **The room's dressing (CANON, golden #10 and #17).** Sodium light
  through venetian blinds, the wall map marked with red op-circles,
  scratched metal desk and chairs. On the desk, the half-built
  "Deep Space Explorer" model kit (Aurora Models, Kit No. 87, 1:1400
  — no Trek branding, by law) and the Earl Grey tin: the Patrick
  Stewart homage, and the room's dressing anchor. **The kit visibly
  progresses across a career** — a second nacelle joined here, a
  paint pot moved there — the career's quiet clock, kept in plastic
  and glue. (Concept staging: the built room will differ in
  dressing; the kit and the tin are the anchors.)

## 5. Greyport — districts and their character (CANON names + PROPOSED flesh)

Canon names (WORLD.md §4, §7): Greyport, an American Atlantic port,
designated the **Greyport Special Administrative District** — dockyards
and container yards under watchtowers, a market arcade that never
entirely closes, bridge checkpoints, a financial district gone grey and
quiet, tenement districts where the curfew is a rumor the poor can't
afford to believe, the old town, the Administration quarter, the market
streets, the temple ground. Scale law: **Greyport is BIG** — multiple
districts, checkpoint crossings between them, rooftops as a second city
laid over the first. **Big means dense, not sprawling:** a city like a
dungeon; every district is a dungeon floor with its own light, its own
patrol economy, its own way of being watched. Slice 1's block is not a
sample of the city; it is the city's TILE — the generative pattern
every district is a variation of. The city exists whole as DATA and
becomes geometry only around the operator; an op witnesses one district
deeply; a career walks the whole map.

District characters below: the first line of each is canon; the rest is
**PROPOSED** flesh, written to the district's canon function, and it
needs Chase's ruling before it hardens into lore.

- **The Dockyards (CANON: watchtowers, container yards, patrol frames).**
  PROPOSED: the city's working throat — cranes, freight shafts, dry
  docks, the night ferries. The mecha's home ground: patrol frames
  stand in plazas like statues of the state. The grapple's best
  country (cranes, wire masts, tower rails) and the witness economy's
  worst (open ground, few phones, long sightlines). The scream of a
  Type 2, if she ever comes here, carries over water.
- **The Old Town (CANON: named district).** PROPOSED: narrow streets,
  tall walls, courtyards as rooms — the dungeon's tightest floor.
  Checkpoint crossings at its bridges; the Neighbour's best ground,
  because everyone here knows everyone and a stranger is a sentence.
- **The Administration Quarter (CANON: named district).** PROPOSED:
  the greyest light in the city — wide streets, floodlit facades,
  siege frames at the bridges, paperwork made architecture. The
  hardest district to be nobody in, and therefore the one where a
  clean checkpoint pass pays most.
- **The Market Streets and Arcade (CANON: market arcade that never
  entirely closes, dead drops in the arcades).** PROPOSED: the city's
  exchange floor — couriers, dead-letter boxes, safe hotels nearby,
  the queue-keeper's beat. The dead drop is a civic institution here;
  so is the informer.
- **The Temple Ground (CANON: the one tolerated temple, the
  Wanderer's ground).** PROPOSED: raked gravel, offerings at the gate,
  patrols that do not enter — tolerance with a budget (see §10). The
  only ground in Greyport where grace is a field and the Hound's
  scent breaks.
- **The Tenements (CANON: curfew as rumor).** PROPOSED: the poor
  districts, where the occupation is thinnest and the witness economy
  is thickest — everyone sees, few have phones, and a body found here
  travels by mouth before it travels by wire.
- **The Financial District (CANON: gone grey and quiet).** PROPOSED:
  empty after curfew, mainframes behind glass, camera circuits — the
  analog SIGINT district, where sweeping a room and following a wire
  is the op.

Foreign stations — the old allies and the old enemies alike — keep
offices the Administration tolerates because it reads their mail
(CANON). Couriers on the night ferries, defectors in the safe hotels,
dead drops in the arcades: the city of spies is a function of the
port, not a decoration on it.

## 6. The mecha program (CANON)

**Walkers, not tanks.** The Regime's machines are bipedal because the
doctrine is presence, not war — a walker can climb a dockyard stair,
stand in a plaza like a statue of the state, and look down. Patrol
frames work the dockyards; siege frames hold the bridges. The concept
art is a patrol frame: weathered grey plate over black machinery, a
cannon for a right arm, one red eye-slit, three bars on the red
shoulder plate (golden #5; rear #8: bare machinery back, insignia
front-only, pauldron-left / cannon-right).

**Type 1 — the walker (CANON, ruled AUTONOMOUS).** No pilot, no control
link, no operator in a van. An onboard analog governor — cams, relays,
gyroscopes, punched doctrine — runs the machine. Consequences, all
canon: there is NO signals kill (nothing to jam, spoof, or negotiate
with); the SIGINT trade reads the machine instead (it radiates and
whines and ranges, and the visor reads its state — searching,
suspicious, locked — from its sounds); its doctrine is FIXED, therefore
learnable to the letter, and the Archive accumulates its
stimulus/response tables op over op; misdirection is physical, never
electronic; the soft kill becomes the doctrine trap — led into the dry
dock, the freight shaft, the street it cannot leave, and held there
until its power dies or the Regime cranes it out. Damage compounds
through the governor: a blinded machine compensates in fixed, readable
ways (leans on audio), so last op's wound is this op's exploit. The
horror is bureaucratic: nobody hesitates inside it, nobody surrenders
it, nobody recalls it. The Regime built a machine because men asked
questions. It does not.

**The kill ladder (CANON).** No health bar, no DPS. Damage is STATE.
(0) Avoid it — the grade rewards never meeting it. (1) Blind and
deafen it — sensors, steam, smoke, bloomed night optics. (2) Hobble it
with the terrain — crane loads, dropped containers, slicks, rubble,
cut street cables. (3) Kill it — always preparation + position, never
a firefight: a placed charge on the back from a rooftop; industrial
machinery (crane drop, dry dock, freight shaft, the fuel depot it is
lured beside); its own cannon, baited. The cannon cannot be destroyed
by the agent's weapons; its fire can be BAITED — the Regime's own gun
turned on its own checkpoint, fuel store, or support. Power/coolant
runs EXPOSED on the bare back (golden #8) because doctrine never
expected one man behind it: the back is the intended kill path,
reached from above; the grapple buys the angle. Persistence: a blinded
eye stays blind until Regime logistics repair it; a soft-killed machine
is RECOVERED and returns; a destroyed one is gone but raises the
city's alert budget; the same unit returns with weld plates over old
wounds. Every hard kill is loud and witnessed, and the record prices
it: a Ghost never kills one. Fairness: it telegraphs (servo noise, the
eye's sweep, ground tremor); its perception runs on the same
light/sound fields as everything else; the grab (golden #12) is
survivable at a cost — a struggle window, gear left in its claw,
injury carried into the record. Never instant death.

**Type 2 — the white one (CANON, name UNDECIDED).** A white, female-
figured, incredibly obese mecha that moves extremely fast and screams
like a soul trapped inside the suit as she charges. The Type 1's
inversion: white plate is always lit under the one-field law (she
cannot use shadow, cannot approach unseen, and does not care); doctrine
is momentum (straight charges, overshoot, cannot climb — rooftops are
refuge); contact is gib-capable, NOT a held grab; the scream is a
district-level stimulus (soldiers orient, citizens scatter, the
Hound's ears go up); her voice apparatus is a silencable component;
she is escalation bought by a risen alert budget, never population —
never a standing patrol, never common; her rarity is a tone law.
Identity render (golden #37): smooth dark faceless dome, speaker
grille in her throat, three bars on a RED field on her LEFT pauldron,
white chipped/rust-streaked. **MYSTERY LAW: what is inside her is
UNRESOLVED by design, and this bible does not resolve it** — volunteer,
prisoner, punishment, or a recording of a woman no longer in there,
the Archive's page is thin and partly unfiled. **Field name
UNDECIDED: THE BRIDE / MOTHER** — both stand as proposals; the story
uses "Type 2" in the Archive's voice and lets the street's names stay
street talk.

**Marks timeline (CANON, thin by design).** They are crewed machines
in the early marks and increasingly automated in the later ones — the
timeline of the marks is a story the Archive is assembling, and it
does not like where the arithmetic points. This bible adds nothing to
that sentence; the campaign's late ops let the player assemble more
of it, never all of it.

## 7. Citizens' life under occupation (CANON)

Greyport is populated, and its population is a system, not set
dressing. Citizens are built as a KIT — base silhouettes varied by
coat, head, and palette — a crowd of individuals, never one cloned
face repeated. They work four jobs in the simulation: **the witness
economy** (a citizen who sees a body, a drawn weapon, or a man
climbing a wall runs for a telephone kiosk — a second stealth clock
the visor never shows: who saw, and how far they are from the nearest
phone); **the checkpoint queue** (a crowd that shuffles, watches, and
remembers faces); **moral weight** (bystanders price the Butcher's
road in the grading — what a street saw cannot be un-seen by the
record); and **ordinary life** on the same perception fields as
everyone else (they shelter from rain, freeze and look down for the
mecha, scatter at gunfire — and at a scream).

Life under the occupation, as the systems price it: papers at bridges,
a curfew the poor cannot afford to believe, queues that watch,
informers built from the same kit as the crowd (the Neighbour is
terrifying because he IS the crowd, with a notebook), and offerings
at the temple gate (the Wanderer is the citizens' saint before she is
the operator's ally). Nobody in this world thinks they are the
villain of it. The Regime's soldiers believe in Order the way the
Agency believes in the Archive. The game believes both of them, and
lets the record argue.

## 8. The Resistance — only what canon supports (CANON, thin)

Canon supports exactly this much: the soldiery hunts **the
resistance** and the Agency alike (DESIGN.md §3); the city holds
defectors in safe hotels and foreign stations the Administration
tolerates because it reads their mail. Canon names no Resistance
organization, leader, doctrine, or structure — and this bible invents
none as settled fact.

**PROPOSED (needs Chase's ruling):** the Resistance stays off-screen
as a pressure in the simulation, never a faction the player joins and
never a cast member with a face. It appears only as the Regime's
paperwork describes it (raid targets, reprisal schedules, descriptions
entered into the other archive) and as systemic-op material (a courier
to tail, a safe hotel to watch, a defector to extract — where the
player's orders come from Control, not from the Resistance). Keeping
it faceless preserves the game's true triangle: the Administration,
the Agency, and the city itself. If Chase wants a Resistance with a
name and a leader, that is a new canon decision, listed in §13.

## 9. The campaign arc — PROPOSED (the whole arc needs Chase's ruling)

Nothing in this section is canon. It is built from canon systems and
is proposed as the spine that interleaves with the systemic city
(DESIGN.md §6: hand-built story ops interleave with systemic ops, and
the systemic city keeps score between them).

**Premise.** The operator arrives in Greyport as the Agency's field
asset with one standing order: build the Archive's case against the
Administration — names, orders, signatures — one operation at a time.
The spine is the case being built. The city is the war that keeps
happening around it. The spine ends; the city does not.

**Escalation law (canon systems, applied).** The campaign escalates
through the game's own economies, not through bigger guns:
- **Alert budget:** rises with witnessed violence, hard mecha kills,
  and failed crossings. A risen budget buys harder patrols, then
  Weathervane coverage of the vertical layer, then — at its peak —
  a Type 2 escalation event. It falls with clean ops, quiet crossings,
  and time. The player feels the city's memory as pressure.
- **Witness economy:** descriptions accumulate in the other archive.
  A Neighbour description active during an op changes what the
  checkpoint reads, what the queue remembers, and whether the
  Wanderer can appear (never under an active description — canon).
- **Grades as currency:** Ghost, Surgeon, Butcher, Ghost-That-Never-
  Was pay the RPG layer (service skills persist; operator skills die
  with the agent) and unlock the spine's later orders — Control
  trusts a clean file with harder work.
- **The kill ladder as curriculum:** the campaign teaches the mecha
  the way the game prices it — avoid, then blind, then hobble, then
  kill — and the Type 2 arrives only after the player has earned the
  right to fear her properly.

**ACT I — LEARNING THE CITY (PROPOSED, 4 story ops).** The operator
learns Greyport the way a professional does: by crossing it.
1. *First Crossing.* A checkpoint pass on foot, papers in order,
   queue watched, rota learned. Teaches: HALT, papers, the queue's
   memory. Grade stakes: a clean pass is the spine's first clean
   line; a detention is the first black mark.
2. *The Dead Letter.* A dead drop in the market arcade, a tail to
   lose, a courier to follow without being followed. Teaches:
   analog SIGINT, the sense view, the tail as a system.
3. *The Cold Store.* A soldier sleeping in a cold store (the
   Keeper's Room concept scene's op, retold as a playable op — the
   scene itself stays concept-only): checkpoint, wall, witness,
   soldier left sleeping, not killed. Teaches: mercy as a system
   (mercy raises the chance the Wanderer appears next — canon),
   bodies as evidence, the witness clock.
4. *The Patrol.* First sustained mecha contact: a patrol frame on a
   dockyard street, to be avoided, read, and left unmet. Teaches:
   the one-field law, the visor's read of emissions, the grab as a
   survivable cost. A Ghost here is the act's thesis: never meeting
   it is the best outcome.

**ACT II — THE ARCHIVE GROWS TEETH (PROPOSED, 5 story ops).**
Systemic ops now interleave heavily; the city's factions move between
story beats. The Hound, the Weathervane, and the Neighbour arrive as
a set, each owning a sense the player thought was safe.
5. *The Scent.* First Hound op: a trail the player cannot watch
   himself spend, rain as the only wash, water as the only break.
   Teaches scent discipline; the debrief grades it after the fact.
6. *The High Ground.* First Weathervane op: the vertical layer
   contested — a rooftop crossing under a scheduled scope, the
   telephone wire as the enemy's voice. Teaches: the grapple crosses
   sightlines, and sightlines are owned.
7. *The Description.* First Neighbour op: the player is watched,
   followed at a distance, written down — and the op's success state
   is not a gunfight but a description entering the other archive.
   Teaches: behaviour as the only tell, and the price of being
   described (the Wanderer cannot appear; checkpoints read harder).
8. *The Blind.* First deliberate mecha wound: blind a patrol frame
   (sensors, steam, smoke) and exploit its fixed compensation next
   op. Teaches: damage as state, persistence (the eye stays blind
   until logistics repair it), the Archive's stimulus/response
   tables as a weapon.
9. *The Hobble.* First terrain kill-or-cripple: a frame led into the
   dry dock, the freight shaft, the street it cannot leave — the
   doctrine trap, held until its power dies or the Regime cranes it
   out. Teaches: the city as the weapon. A hard kill here raises the
   alert budget enough to buy Act III's event.

**ACT III — THE RECKONING (PROPOSED, 4 story ops).**
10. *The Escalation.* The risen alert budget buys her: first Type 2
    contact. Momentum, overshoot, rooftops as refuge, the scream as
    a district-level stimulus. The op's win is not killing her — it
    is surviving her, silencing her voice apparatus, and learning
    the charge line. Her name is never settled in the op's text.
11. *The Sword.* The skill tree's final unlock, earned at the tree's
    end and never before: a blade that makes no report. The op that
    earns it is the quietest in the game — arm's length is the whole
    of its reach, and closing that distance is the risk. **MYSTERY
    LAW: its provenance points at the temple and stops there; the
    Archive's page is marked UNRESOLVED, and the game does not
    explain it.**
12. *The Coolant Line.* The kill ladder's top rung, executed: from
    above, by grapple, onto the bare machinery of a Type 1's back —
    a blade through the coolant line. The op is preparation +
    position, never a firefight, and the record prices it exactly:
    a hard kill, loud, witnessed, and final for that unit.
13. *The Case Leaves the City.* The spine's end: the Archive's case
    — names, orders, signatures, the assembled stimulus/response
    tables, the welded plates and the descriptions — is smuggled
    out of Greyport on the night ferries, through the market
    arcade's dead-letter infrastructure, under the Administration's
    own paperwork. The operator does not leave with it. The
    operator stays, because the city stays. The final debrief is
    the handler reading back the whole career, grade by grade, and
    the file closing on a sentence the player earned: Ghost,
    Surgeon, Butcher, or the Ghost-That-Never-Was.

**How the campaign ends (PROPOSED).** The spine closes; the city
does not. After *The Case Leaves the City*, systemic ops continue
indefinitely — tails, dead drops, extractions, eliminations,
burglaries of secrets, counter-surveillance sweeps, composed by the
simulation itself — and the service record keeps growing. A new
career (after a burned agent) resumes the spine from the Archive's
record, with the city's grudges intact and the predecessor's file on
the desk: the successor inherits the city, the Archive, and the
unfinished business — but not the dead man's operator skills (RPG
law: operator layer dies with the agent; service layer persists).
The campaign's end is therefore a file closed, not a world ended —
which is the only ending this design's laws allow.

**What the campaign never does (LAW).** It never resolves the
mysteries of §11. It never makes the Handler Control. It never opens
the pod, names the Type 2's passenger, explains the nightjars, or
gives the Wanderer a motive or the sword a receipt. The story is
built to carry these unanswered: each appears, each matters, none is
explained — and the debrief's silence about them is itself the
record's honesty.

assault visor DARK and never glows (red light is the mecha's
language); NSA sleeve patch DELETED — no labels, letters, or
insignia on him in either configuration in-game (an exile service
does not sign its work). **OPEN (canon):** whether the in-game model
wears patch + balaclava at all times vs the identity governing
presentation — rides to the model pass. Priced: assault cannot pass
a checkpoint; witnessed assault description is the most expensive
in the witness economy. Signature: the HK USP, the Agency's standard
sidearm, carried in every op, the one constant in the kit. Full
armoury: golden #36, eight weapons in progression order (service
pistol, suppressed SMG — the only quiet firearm — field carbine,
battle rifle, pump shotgun, marksman rifle, squad automatic,
single-shot launcher whose concussion blinds and hobbles a mecha;
the kill still belongs to the coolant line). Final unlock: the
samurai sword (see §9, Act III, and the mystery law).

### The Handler (CANON)

The debrief officer given a body (golden #18 front, #19 rear): gaunt,
weathered, grey at the temples, deep-set eyes that have read too many
files. Charcoal checker-wool greatcoat over a three-piece suit,
leather gloves, brass-clasped briefcase in his RIGHT hand in both
views — the Archive's paperwork made portable. Voice: the narrator's
— gruff, harsh, weathered, slow, methodical. He belongs to the room,
the desk, and the record; he is not a field character. **MYSTERY LAW:
whether he is Control or only its face is deliberately unstated.**
The game never answers it; a debrief may contain one line — "You were
seen sitting with someone at ——. The record will omit it. This
once." — and whether that protects her, the operator, or the
handler's own file is unstated, like his nature.

### The Wanderer (CANON)

The operator's one ally, and the city's one unexplained person: a
Japanese Zen monastic (canon documents use both "monk" and "nun" for
her; this bible uses "monastic" and flags the wording variance in
§12), who shows herself rarely and belongs to no side's machinery.
The NSA does not run her; nobody runs her. The Archive holds a folder
on her with a single page — her description, marked UNRESOLVED. The
Regime's other archive holds the same page. She is the one person in
Greyport with no file, in a city made of files. Golden #27/#28: elderly,
shaved head, patched dark kesa, wooden staff, prayer beads, gaze
passing by the camera entirely. Appearance is doctrine, learnable like
the mecha's: she appears after mercy (an op where the operator aided
the wounded or spared a man raises the chance she is somewhere quiet
in the next); she tends the badly wounded operator when he is alone —
the game's only outside aid, priced in TIME against the op clock;
never during alarm, never twice in one op, never under an active
Neighbour description. Grace is a field, like light: near her,
soldiers' suspicion rises slower; the Hound will not track across her
raked gravel; the Neighbour lowers his notebook; in the visor's sense
view she barely registers — no emitter, no signal. Silence, read as
data. Her ground: the Regime tolerates one temple as a pressure valve
and a propaganda of tolerance; patrols do not enter. That tolerance
has a BUDGET — use her cover too often and the temple gets watched,
then closed. She aids ANY wounded, enemy included. Across a career
she can die; the successor inherits the garden, the raked gravel, and
the systems, as her ghost. **MYSTERY LAW: what she wants is never
answered** — defector of the Emergency years, Archive founder's last
agent, or simply a monk — the game does not say whether she is any
of these, and the mystery is that there is none.

### Mister Ed (CANON)

The fable Greyport tells about itself (golden #41): a grey,
long-limbed being in work clothes, boots up on the desk, pipe lit, in
a room on no district plan, keeping the EliteDesk 800 G1 that keeps
the world. Inside its case, where the drives should be, an amber pod
grows around something fetal and patient, placarded SYSTEMZ. His
doctrine is maintenance, never intervention: he does not save, warn,
or grade the operator — he keeps the lamps lit and the rain falling
and the districts stitched, and the proof of his shift is that the
world is still there in the morning. His traces are repair-shaped: a
quarter that stuttered and recovered, damage the city quietly healed
overnight, weather that restarts like someone re-seated a card. He is
never met and never fought. If a player ever finds the room, the
chair is warm, the pipe is lit, and he is not in it. **MYSTERY LAW:
what grows in the pod is unresolved by design.** Out of fiction the
fable is simply true: the world runs on an EliteDesk 800 G1.

### The Hound — and his handler (CANON)

A scent-tracking dog worked by a handler (a soldier variant: same
greatcoat family, long tracking lead, coiled second line, canvas
scent bag at the belt). The dog: a large dark-sable shepherd, erect
ears, unmuzzled when working, in a leather-and-webbing Regime harness
with a small three-bars plate on the LEFT shoulder strap (golden
#20–#22: pure left-profile identity, alert three-quarter, rear
three-quarter with head turned catching a scent). Regime paperwork: a
scent asset with a serial number, Tracking Section. The Archive's
name: the Hound. The system it adds is SCENT — the third perception
channel, and the only one the operator cannot watch himself spend.
His trail persists on the ground he crosses and decays over minutes;
crossing his own trail freshens it; rain washes it; wind carries it;
water breaks it. The dog's state reads from posture alone: nose down
and pulling = tracking; head up, still = scent held; hackles, line
straining = contact. The visor cannot show the player's own scent
picture — the debrief grades it after the fact. Killing the dog is
priced in the record like everything; the handler's grief is the most
human thing the Regime owns, and the game lets it be witnessed.

### The Weathervane (CANON)

A rooftop observer (golden #23 front, #24 rear): scope, field
telephone, patience. Ear-flaps down, long brass scope, logbook under
his arm, no rifle; from behind, the canvas telephone pack and the wire
spool at his left side, the black wire paying out across the floor and
out of frame — he is wired to the street he watches. He almost never
shoots — he REPORTS, and the street below answers (a telephone call
is a perception event the whole district hears the consequences of).
He owns the high sightlines the grapple crosses, contesting the
vertical layer; his hearing is poor, his rotation is scheduled, his
silhouette against the sky is his tell. Regime: Observation Post
(Elevated). The Archive: the Weathervane — he turns to face whatever
the street is doing.

### The Neighbour (CANON)

A plain-clothes informer (golden #25 front, #26 rear): no uniform, no
weapon but a notebook and the telephone kiosk on the corner. A nobody
in a damp raincoat, notebook open, pencil mid-note, looking directly
into the camera — the only member of the cast who looks at YOU. From
behind he is anyone at all: shopping bag, umbrella, the spiral pad
riding in his coat pocket as the single tell. NO insignia anywhere on
him, by law. Unreadable by dress — readable only by behaviour (he
watches, he follows at a distance, he writes). His success state is
not a gunfight: it is your description, entered into the other
archive. The social layer's enemy, and the checkpoint system's shadow.
Regime: Block Warden (Volunteer). The Archive: the Neighbour —
anyone's. Built from the citizen kit on purpose: the informer is
terrifying because he is the crowd, with a notebook.

### The Citizens — the dockhand, the queue-keeper (CANON)

Founding pair of the citizen kit (golden #29, #30). **NPC Male 1, the
dockhand:** broad, patched, a lunch tin in his hand, a face that at
twenty metres in rain could be mistaken for the Neighbour's — a
confusion the design prices in on purpose. **NPC Female 1, the
queue-keeper:** belted raincoat, headscarf, string bag, purse in both
hands, and the assessing sideways look of the best witness in the
game. Both work the four citizen jobs (§7): witness economy,
checkpoint queue, moral weight, ordinary life. The Wanderer is the
citizens' saint before she is the operator's ally — the offerings at
her temple gate are theirs.

### The Soldier and the Type 1 (CANON, dossier form)

The soldier: see §3. The Type 1 walker: see §6. Both are dossier
entries in the Archive's voice (see COMPENDIUM.md): the soldier as a
doctrine with a face the player learns to read; the walker as a
machine with a stimulus/response table the player learns to predict.
Neither is a character with a motive — that is the design: the
Regime's instruments are systems, and systems are learned, not
befriended.

### Type 2 — the Bride/Mother question (CANON, name UNDECIDED)

See §6. In dossier form: the Archive's entry is TYPE 2, thin and
partly unfiled. The street's names — THE BRIDE (for the white),
MOTHER (for the shape and the sound of her coming) — are standing
proposals, undecided, and this bible does not pick one. The story
uses "Type 2" wherever the Archive speaks and lets the street talk
both names. **MYSTERY LAW: what is inside her is not answered, in
any dossier, op, or debrief line.**

## 11. Mysteries — the law, stated once, binding everywhere

These are unresolved BY DESIGN, not by omission. No story beat, lore
entry, dossier, debrief line, or compendium paragraph in this bible —
or in any work built from it — resolves them:

1. What is inside the Type 2.
2. Whether the Handler is Control or only its face.
3. The Wanderer's motive (and whether she has one the game could name).
4. What grows in Mister Ed's pod.
5. The sword's provenance (it points at the Wanderer and stops there).
6. Why the nightjars are there.

The nightjars' own laws (CANON): rare — a handful in the whole city,
perched on wires, crane arms, rooftop edges; most players will finish
an op having seen none. Heard before seen — their eerie churring call
carries at night between the cranes, a sound-engine event with no
source marker; the visor does not tag it. A collectible — each
nightjar found is counted in the service record's margin, never
explained, never cashed in for power. Mystery is the mechanic: no lore
entry resolves them; the handler does not remark on them; the Archive
holds no page. (Out-of-world: the nightjar is the lab's own mark —
NIGHTWARE LABZ' bird, sitting inside its game unexplained, exactly as
a signature should be.)

The story is built to carry these unanswered: each mystery appears,
each matters, none is explained — and the debrief's silence about
them is itself the record's honesty.
